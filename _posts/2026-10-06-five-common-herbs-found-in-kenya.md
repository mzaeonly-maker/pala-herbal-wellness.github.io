# 5 Common Herbs Found in Kenya and Their Traditional Uses

Kenya has a rich tradition of using plants for food, cultural practices, household remedies and general wellbeing. Some of these plants are found in home gardens, farms, markets and even growing naturally around communities.

Herbs and other medicinal plants have attracted interest because they contain naturally occurring compounds that can affect the human body. However, the fact that a plant is natural does not automatically mean that it is safe, effective for every condition, or suitable for everyone.

In this article, Pala Herbal Wellness explores five familiar plants that are commonly encountered in Kenya: **aloe vera, moringa, lemongrass, ginger and neem**.

The purpose is educational. We will look at how these plants have traditionally been used, how people commonly prepare them, what scientific research tells us, and what safety issues should be considered.

---

## 1. Aloe Vera

### What is aloe vera?

Aloe vera is a succulent plant with thick, fleshy leaves containing a clear inner gel. It grows well in warm and relatively dry environments and is familiar to many households because of its traditional use for skin care.

The leaf is not simply one substance. It contains different parts, including the inner gel and the yellowish latex found closer to the outer portion of the leaf. This distinction is important because these parts can have different effects on the body.

Aloe products may therefore differ considerably depending on which part of the leaf has been used and how it has been processed.

### Traditional uses

Traditionally, aloe has been associated particularly with skin care. The clear inner gel has been applied to the skin in various communities for minor skin discomfort and irritation.

Aloe has also been used traditionally in preparations intended for digestive purposes. However, traditional use should not automatically be interpreted as proof that aloe can treat a particular disease.

Research suggests that topical aloe gel may have some usefulness for certain skin applications, including some burns, although evidence varies depending on the condition and the product used. 

### How aloe is commonly prepared

For external use, people traditionally use the clear gel from the inside of a freshly cut leaf.

A simple traditional approach is:

1. Select a healthy aloe leaf.
2. Wash the outside thoroughly.
3. Cut the leaf carefully.
4. Allow the yellow latex to drain away.
5. Remove the outer leaf and collect the clear inner gel.
6. Wash the gel before external application.

The important point is that **the clear gel and aloe latex should not be treated as the same thing**.

### What does science say?

Aloe has been investigated for several health applications. Some clinical research suggests topical aloe gel may help with certain burns and may have other potential skin-related uses. However, evidence is not strong enough to support every traditional or commercial claim made about aloe. 

### Aloe safety

This is where caution becomes especially important.

Oral aloe latex can cause abdominal cramps and diarrhea. Some oral whole-leaf preparations have also raised significant safety concerns. Aloe taken by mouth may also interact with medicines. 

Aloe taken orally should also be avoided during pregnancy and breastfeeding unless a qualified healthcare professional specifically advises otherwise. 

**Key lesson:** Aloe gel used on the skin is very different from drinking homemade whole-leaf aloe preparations.

## 2. Moringa

### What is moringa?

Moringa, commonly known as the **drumstick tree**, is a hardy plant that grows in many tropical and subtropical regions.

Its leaves are particularly valued as a food because they contain nutrients and can be incorporated into meals. Moringa leaves may be cooked like other leafy vegetables or dried and ground into powder.

Because of its nutritional value, moringa is sometimes described as a "miracle plant." Such descriptions should be treated carefully. A nutritious plant can contribute to a healthy diet without being a cure for disease.

### Traditional uses

Moringa leaves have traditionally been used as food and in household preparations. Different communities may prepare the leaves as vegetables, teas or powders.

Traditional knowledge surrounding moringa may include its use for general strength, nutrition and wellbeing.

The nutritional role of moringa is particularly important because the plant can provide a useful source of plant-based nutrients when incorporated into a varied diet.

### How moringa may be prepared as food

Fresh moringa leaves can be:

- Washed carefully.
- Removed from the tougher stems.
- Cooked with other vegetables.
- Added to soups or stews.
- Dried and powdered for later culinary use.

Moringa leaf powder can also be mixed into foods such as porridge or vegetables.

However, adding more powder does not necessarily mean greater health benefits. Concentrated products can behave differently from ordinary food.

### What should we understand about the evidence?

Research into moringa has investigated nutritional properties and possible effects on several health outcomes. However, evidence from laboratory or animal studies should not automatically be interpreted as proof that moringa treats diseases in humans.

This is an important principle when reading information about herbal products:

**A promising laboratory result is not the same as a proven medical treatment.**

### Moringa safety

Moringa leaves used as food are different from concentrated extracts or supplements.

People taking medicines, pregnant women, breastfeeding mothers, children and people with existing medical conditions should be cautious about concentrated herbal products and discuss their use with a qualified healthcare professional.

The safest educational approach is to think of ordinary moringa leaves primarily as a food ingredient rather than assuming that concentrated moringa products can replace medical treatment.

## 3. Lemongrass

### What is lemongrass?

Lemongrass is an aromatic grass recognized by its long narrow leaves and distinctive lemon-like fragrance.

It is commonly used as a culinary and herbal tea plant. The pleasant aroma comes from naturally occurring volatile compounds in the plant.

Because lemongrass grows well in warm climates, it can be cultivated in household gardens and small farms.

### Traditional uses

Lemongrass tea is traditionally enjoyed as a warm beverage. In different cultures, lemongrass preparations have also been associated with digestive comfort and general relaxation.

Its pleasant citrus-like flavour makes it particularly popular as a tea ingredient.

### A simple tea preparation

For a mild household tea, people commonly:

1. Wash fresh lemongrass thoroughly.
2. Cut the leaves or stems into smaller pieces.
3. Place a small amount in hot water.
4. Allow it to steep for several minutes.
5. Strain before drinking.

Fresh or dried lemongrass can be used.

It is better to begin with a mild preparation rather than making extremely concentrated herbal drinks.

### Lemongrass is not the same as lemongrass essential oil

This distinction is extremely important.

A cup of lemongrass tea and a concentrated essential oil are completely different preparations.

Essential oils contain concentrated chemical constituents and should not be treated like ordinary herbal tea. They should not be swallowed casually or used internally without appropriate professional guidance.

### What should readers remember?

Traditional tea use may provide a pleasant way of enjoying the plant, but it should not be presented as a guaranteed treatment for infections, diabetes, high blood pressure, cancer or other serious diseases.

When an illness is persistent, severe or worsening, proper medical assessment is more important than relying on herbal tea alone.

## 4. Ginger

### What is ginger?

Ginger is an aromatic plant whose underground stem, known as a rhizome, is widely used as a food spice and herbal ingredient.

It has a strong, warm flavour and can be used fresh, dried or powdered.

Ginger has a long history of traditional use for digestive complaints and other everyday health concerns. Modern research has also examined ginger for several specific conditions. 

### Traditional uses

Ginger is commonly used traditionally for:

- Digestive discomfort.
- Nausea.
- Feeling cold or congested.
- General warming drinks.
- Flavouring herbal teas.

One of the better-studied uses of ginger is nausea and vomiting. Research suggests ginger may help with nausea associated with pregnancy, although anyone who is pregnant should discuss herbal supplements with a healthcare professional. 

Research has also investigated ginger for menstrual cramps and symptoms associated with knee osteoarthritis, although the quality and strength of evidence varies. 

### Simple ginger tea

A common household preparation is ginger tea.

A simple method is:

1. Wash a fresh piece of ginger.
2. Peel it if desired.
3. Slice or grate a small amount.
4. Add it to hot water.
5. Allow it to steep for several minutes.
6. Strain and drink while warm.

Some people add lemon or other food ingredients for flavour.

This is a food-style preparation and should not be confused with concentrated ginger supplements.

### Ginger and modern research

Ginger is one of the more extensively studied herbs on this list.

According to the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, research has found possible benefits for some forms of nausea and vomiting, and ginger supplements may help reduce the severity of menstrual cramps. Evidence for other uses is more uncertain. 

This demonstrates an important lesson in herbal education:

**One plant can have several traditional uses, but scientific evidence may support only some of them.**

### Ginger safety

Ginger can cause side effects in some people, including stomach discomfort, heartburn, diarrhea and irritation of the mouth or throat. 

People taking medicines should discuss regular use of concentrated ginger products with a healthcare professional because herbs can interact with medicines.

## 5. Neem

### What is neem?

Neem is a tree widely known in tropical regions. Different parts of the plant—including leaves, bark and seeds—have a long history of traditional use.

Neem is particularly interesting because it has been used for household, agricultural and traditional wellness purposes.

However, neem should not be treated as one single preparation. Different parts of the tree contain different compounds, and extracts can be considerably more concentrated than ordinary plant material.

### Traditional uses

Neem leaves and other parts of the tree have traditionally been used in various communities for skin care, hygiene and other household practices.

Neem is also widely associated with traditional approaches to managing insects and agricultural pests.

Some people prepare neem leaves as washes or other traditional preparations.

### Why concentration matters

One of the biggest mistakes people can make with herbal products is assuming that a stronger preparation must be better.

That is not necessarily true.

A plant contains many chemical compounds. Concentrating those compounds can also increase the possibility of unwanted effects.

For this reason, homemade concentrated neem extracts should not automatically be considered safer than regulated products.

### Neem and serious diseases

Neem is sometimes promoted online as a treatment for conditions such as diabetes, infections or cancer.

Such claims should be approached very carefully.

Laboratory studies can identify interesting biological activity, but laboratory findings do not prove that a plant can safely treat a disease in humans.

Anyone with a serious medical condition should seek appropriate medical care rather than replacing prescribed treatment with neem.

# Understanding the Difference Between Traditional Use and Medical Evidence

One of the most important skills in herbal education is learning to separate **traditional knowledge, scientific evidence and marketing claims**.

These three things are not identical.

### Traditional knowledge

Traditional knowledge is information passed between generations through communities and cultural practices.

It can be valuable and deserves respect. However, traditional use alone does not establish the correct dose, safety profile, effectiveness or suitability for every person.

### Scientific evidence

Scientific evidence comes from systematic investigation.

Researchers may study a plant in laboratories, animals or humans. Human clinical trials generally provide more directly relevant information about whether a particular preparation may help with a particular condition.

Even when studies exist, researchers must consider their quality, size, design and limitations.

### Marketing claims

Marketing claims are statements made to sell or promote a product.

A product described as "natural," "traditional," "detoxifying," or "powerful" has not automatically been proven effective or safe.

Consumers should therefore learn to ask:

- What exactly is in the product?
- Which part of the plant was used?
- How concentrated is it?
- What evidence supports the claim?
- Are there known side effects?
- Could it interact with medicines?
- Who should avoid it?

# Why Preparation Matters

The same plant can behave very differently depending on how it is prepared.

Consider these examples:

**Fresh ginger in food** is not the same as a concentrated ginger extract.

**Aloe skin gel** is not the same as aloe latex taken by mouth.

**Moringa leaves used as food** are not the same as a highly concentrated supplement.

**Lemongrass tea** is not the same as concentrated lemongrass essential oil.

**Neem leaves** are not necessarily equivalent to a concentrated neem extract.

This is why simply saying "I used the herb" does not provide enough information to judge safety.

The plant species, plant part, preparation method, concentration, amount and route of administration all matter.

# A Simple Guide to Using Herbal Products Responsibly

Before using an unfamiliar herbal product, consider these seven questions.

### 1. Do I know the plant correctly?

Several plants can look similar. Misidentification can result in the wrong plant being consumed.

### 2. Which part of the plant am I using?

Leaves, roots, bark, seeds, flowers, latex and essential oils can have very different chemical properties.

### 3. How was it prepared?

Tea, food, powder, tincture, extract and essential oil are not interchangeable.

### 4. How much am I taking?

A traditional preparation may be much less concentrated than a commercial extract.

### 5. Am I taking medication?

Herbs and medicines can interact. People should tell healthcare professionals about herbal products they use, particularly when taking medicines with a narrow safety range. 

### 6. Am I in a group requiring extra caution?

Pregnant women, breastfeeding mothers, children, older adults and people with chronic medical conditions may require additional guidance.

### 7. Am I using the herb instead of necessary medical care?

This is perhaps the most important question.

Herbal products may have a place in food, traditional practices and complementary approaches, but they should not be used as an excuse to delay diagnosis or treatment of a serious illness.

# Final Thoughts

Kenya's plant heritage provides an enormous opportunity for learning.

Aloe vera, moringa, lemongrass, ginger and neem demonstrate how plants can be part of food traditions, household practices and cultural knowledge. They also demonstrate why responsible herbal education matters.

Some plants have been studied extensively, while others require much more research. Even when a plant shows promise, the evidence may apply only to a particular preparation, dose or health condition.

The best approach is therefore neither to dismiss traditional herbal knowledge nor to believe every claim made about natural remedies.

Instead, we can **learn from tradition, examine the evidence, understand the risks and make informed decisions**.

That is the approach we aim to encourage at Pala Herbal Wellness.

> **Natural does not automatically mean harmless, and traditional use does not automatically mean medically proven. Good herbal knowledge requires both respect for traditional wisdom and careful attention to evidence and safety.**
